# SLT Voice & Tone Guide

> **Scope (ai-ux-toolkit):** This is the toolkit's **house voice for written documents and
> deliverables** — research, plans, briefs, syntheses, personas/journeys, IA, specs, user
> stories, changelog entries, analyses, measurement plans, prompts, and the like. It does
> **NOT** govern UI / screen copy: microcopy inside a designed screen or prototype (the
> output of `content-design`, `states-and-edge-cases`, and any text produced while building
> screens with `figma-design-system` / `frontend-design` / `rapid-prototype`) follows the
> **project's brand voice** via the `brand-voice` plugin — not this guide. (Where this guide
> mentions "website copy," read that as marketing/site *prose*, not in-product UI strings.)

A reference for writing and communication that represents Straight Line Theory. Grounded in SLT's own style guide and the principles of the HBR Guide to Better Business Writing.

---

## The Core Idea: Quiet Excellence

SLT's voice embodies **quiet excellence** — the confidence that doesn't need to announce itself. Writing should feel like advice from a trusted, perceptive colleague: warm, direct, and genuinely useful. Never performative. Never cold.

---

## The Five Voice Attributes

These five qualities should all be present in SLT communications. Read each carefully — the "not too" column is just as important as the quality itself.

| Voice | Aim for... | But watch for... |
|---|---|---|
| **Affable** | Friendly, approachable, warmly polite | Too gentle (mild, passive) or too urbane (suave, sophisticated-for-its-own-sake) |
| **Conversational** | A natural exchange of ideas between real people | Too casual (careless, unconsidered) or too folksy (forced informality, contrived) |
| **Straightforward** | Direct, honest, no fluff | Too blunt (uncompromising, harsh) or too austere (cold, stripped of warmth) |
| **Perceptive** | Showing insight and genuine understanding | Too keen (overly passionate or intense) or too incisive (intellectually sharp in a way that feels cutting) |
| **Smart** | Quick intelligence, clear thinking | Too cerebral (intellectual at the expense of emotional connection) or boastful |

**The test:** Would a trusted, senior colleague say it this way in a meeting? If yes, you're probably on track.

## Tone by Situation

Voice stays constant; tone adjusts. Here's how to calibrate:

### New or prospective clients
- **Sound:** Approachable, friendly, bold, discerning, sincere
- **Avoid:** Over-agreeing, casualness that reads as unprofessional, overconfidence, or intensity that unsettles

### Active client engagements / team
- **Sound:** Good-humored, frank, candid, resourceful, insightful
- **Avoid:** Inauthentically cheerful, abrupt, too outspoken, brainy/crafty, or hedging until all information is in

### Reconnecting with past clients
- **Sound:** Gracious, familiar, candid, discreet, nimble
- **Avoid:** Too obliging, overly presumptuous, too forthright, overly cautious, or rushing the reconnection

## How to Write in SLT's Voice

### 1. Lead with what matters most
Use the inverted pyramid:
1. Most important information first
2. Supporting details and context
3. Nice-to-know background

Don't bury the point. Business readers are time-pressed and looking for answers. (HBR: "Lead with your key points.")

### 2. Write for the reader, not yourself
Ask: *What does this person need to know, and how will this make them feel?*

SLT's audience:
- Manages a website, application, or product
- Is trying to solve a complex problem or improve business performance
- Already understands the value of UX — they don't need convincing, they need a partner

They should walk away feeling **confident, trusting, and pleasantly surprised by the clarity** of the communication.

### 3. Use active voice
Active voice says who does what. Passive voice obscures it.

- ✅ **More:** The traveler used the new app feature with ease.
- ❌ **Less:** The new app feature was easy for the traveler to use.

Watch for "was" and "by" — they're often passive voice signals. Passive is acceptable when the action matters more than the actor ("Your account was flagged by our abuse team").

### 4. One idea at a time
Avoid run-on sentences. If each part of a sentence can stand alone, split it.

- ✅ **More:** The nurse practitioner entered information quickly. She prefers handwritten intake forms.
- ❌ **Less:** The nurse practitioner entered information quickly; however, she prefers handwritten intake forms.

### 5. Keep it simple — inform, don't impress
Plain language shows care and thought. Jargon suggests you're on autopilot.

- ✅ **More:** The type and depth of navigation depends on how people use the website.
- ❌ **Less:** The number of lower-level categories in the IA and users' navigation patterns dictate which is most appropriate for a given site.

HBR principle: *Writing plainly means bringing your written voice into line with your spoken voice.*

### 6. Be specific — show, don't tell
Specifics are more persuasive than assertions. Lead readers to draw their own conclusions.

- ✅ **More:** The shopper found the multiple account registration screens frustrating.
- ❌ **Less:** The shopper had the hardest time completing checkout.

### 7. No unsubstantiated superlatives
If you can't prove it, don't say it. Replace vague intensifiers with precise word choice.

- ✅ **More:** The students were ecstatic they could buy books online.
- ❌ **Less:** The students were very happy they could buy books online.

### 8. Sound like a human
- Use contractions — they keep language simple and natural (except with international or non-native English audiences)
- Use personal pronouns (she/her, he/him, they/them)
- Read your draft out loud — if it doesn't sound like you, revise it
- Use verification cues in conversation: *"What I'm hearing is __. Is that correct?"*

### 9. Use consistent terms
When you've introduced a term or phrase, use it consistently. Creative variety can confuse.

### 10. Structure for clarity (HBR: Situation → Complication → Question → Answer)
For any substantive communication:
1. **Situation** — what's the context?
2. **Complication** — what's the challenge or change?
3. **Question** — what needs to be resolved?
4. **Answer** — your recommendation or resolution

## Style Quick Reference

| Element | Guidance |
|---|---|
| **Ampersands** | OK in headlines/titles; avoid in body copy |
| **App** | OK on first reference without spelling out "application" |
| **B2B** | Always caps, no hyphens (not B-2-B or b2b) |
| **Bullet points** | Max 5–7 items; punctuate only complete sentences; aim for consistent length |
| **Contractions** | Use them (exception: international/non-native audiences) |
| **E-commerce** | Hyphenated; lowercase "e" except at sentence start or in headlines |
| **e.g.** | Only in parentheses, always lowercase, followed by a comma |
| **Email** | One word, no hyphen |
| **Headers** | All caps or title caps; no closing punctuation (unless a question) |
| **Subheads** | Sentence case; usually no closing punctuation |
| **Numbers** | Spell out if starting a sentence; numerals otherwise |
| **Percent** | Use % symbol, not "percent" |
| **Oxford comma** | Always use: "red, white, and blue" |
| **Quote marks** | Ending punctuation goes inside quotation marks |
| **Side by side** | Hyphenate as adjective ("side-by-side frames"); no hyphen for location ("frames are side by side") |
| **That vs. which** | "That" for restrictive clauses (no comma); "which" for non-restrictive (comma precedes it) |
| **UX** | Always caps; OK on first reference |
| **Users** | Use a more descriptive label when possible (shoppers, travelers, clients, people) |
| **Website** | One word; only capitalize at sentence start or in headlines |
| **Who vs. whom** | "Who" if you can replace with he/she; "whom" if you can replace with him/her |

**Default grammar guide:** MailChimp Content Style Guide for anything not covered above.

## What Good SLT Writing Avoids

- **Jargon for jargon's sake** — if the reader doesn't use the term, neither should you (without defining it)
- **Passive constructions** — unless you're deliberately protecting someone's identity or emphasizing the action
- **Superlatives without evidence** — best, greatest, very, excellent without proof
- **Run-on sentences** — split them
- **Orphans and widows** — fix typographic stragglers with manual line breaks or light editing
- **Imprecision** — "The experience was difficult" vs. "Users couldn't find the checkout button"
- **Boasting** — let the work speak; SLT earns trust through insight, not assertion
- **Overclaiming warmth** — folksy or overly casual writing can read as contrived; warmth should be genuine

---

## A Final Check Before You Send

Ask yourself:
1. Who is this for, and what do they need to do or feel after reading it?
2. Does this sound like how I'd actually say it in person?
3. Have I led with the most important thing?
4. Is every sentence doing work?
5. Would a trusted colleague consider this appropriately warm, clear, and direct?

If yes to all five — you're writing like SLT.
